California has a set of standards that include listening comprehension as a skill all students should have to succeed in school. This lesson explores the strategies teachers can apply to develop listening comprehension skills in students.

ELA and ELD Together

Adil is a student from the Middle East living in California, and his first language is Arabic. Since Adil is an English learner, he has two big challenges as a student in California. The first is to meet the state's standards regarding English Language Development (ELD). The second challenge is to use English as a tool to succeed in English Language Arts (ELA).

The ELA/ELD Framework for California public schools is the set of guidelines that define standards that all English speakers must achieve. One of those standards is listening comprehension, which is the skill that allows students to grasp meaning out of the information they hear, then be able to respond appropriately.

Let's look at the strategies teachers have to assist students with listening comprehension.

Strategies for Listening Comprehension

To see how Adil's teachers help him develop his listening comprehension skills, we have a few practical examples. All of the strategies in this section assist students with listening comprehension to succeed both at ELA and ELD.

Listening and Responding

Any listening exercise your students do should always be followed up with an assignment that leads students to provide a response. Your students can give two types of responses:

Verbal

A verbal response requires your students to orally answer a question or a prompt to make a comment, give an opinion, etc.

For example, Adil's teacher reads aloud to students about a boy who learns to play basketball after school. The teacher asks Adil: 'Why is the boy enrolled in a basketball program?' Adil tells the teacher, 'Because his dream is to become a professional basketball player.' Verbal responses allow you to check immediately after a listening exercise to see if your students comprehend the information.

Nonverbal

A nonverbal response can be in the form of drawing, taking a multiple choice quiz, etc. In short, nonverbal responses do not require students to orally give answers, while still allowing the teacher to check for comprehension.

For instance, Adil listens to an audio track about a woman who loves pottery. Afterwards, Adil works on a 'True/False' exercise that asks questions about what he just heard.

Listening for Main Ideas

You can ask students to listen for the main idea within a piece of information. For example, Adil's teacher shows students a video clip from the local news. The reporter in the video alerts people in the community about a bear that has been seen in some home yards. When Adil listens, he catches the main idea of the clip, which is the danger the bear poses for people's safety.

When you assign your students a task and ask them to listen only for the main idea, students learn to grasp the key component of a listening piece.

Listening for Details and Sequences

You can also assign your students the task of listening for details and sequences. For example, Adil's teacher tells students they are going to listen to an audio track explaining the steps to make a volcano at home. Afterwards, the teacher asks the students questions to make sure they understood. Questions about details can begin with 'what, when, where, who.' Questions about sequences can include 'What is the first step...? What is necessary after...?' etc.

Listening for Implied Meaning

Similar to the skill of interpreting what an author means, you can ask your students to listen for implied meaning. The questions you might ask include 'What does the speaker mean by...? Why do you think the speaker said that...?'

To illustrate, Adil listens to a lady who says 'As soon as I heard the glass come down very loudly in the living room, I came out of the kitchen and saw the boy next door running quickly towards his house.' The teacher asks, 'What is the implied meaning of what the woman says?' Adil replies, 'That it is possible the boy next door deliberately broke her window.' In short, your students learn to interpret something that is not directly stated, but implied through a hint or suggestion.

When working on implied meaning, it is important that you also define what a speaker means if they're using an idiomatic expression. For example, if Adil hears the expression 'piece of cake' when listening to a dialogue and you already went over that expression, he'll be able to deduce that it means that something is very easy.

Applying Knowledge of Vocabulary

Once your students understand the vocabulary you're going over, you can assign a listening task that includes it. For example, Adil just learned about a few words that relate to how the human heart pumps blood. Now the teacher gives students a listening task that includes those words.

When you give students a listening piece so they apply their knowledge of vocabulary, your students get to put the vocabulary in context. Also, learned vocabulary really sticks through listening.

Discourse Markers and Organization

Your students can practice identifying discourse markers (e.g. 'so, well, I mean') that allow ideas to be connected and organized. For instance, Adil listens to a person giving out the recipe to make a fantastic pumpkin pie. Adil identifies some discourse markers, such as 'first, then, after that,' etc.

Grasping Tone

As you know, the importance of getting the right tone when listening can make the difference between harmony and a misunderstanding. For example, Adil's teacher plays the reactions of different people when they find out they won a prize. Adil identifies disbelief in some cases, joy in others, or extreme excitement. In short, your students should be able to tell what emotions come up through the tone in which things are said.

Lesson Summary

The ELA/ELD Framework for California public schools includes the standard of listening comprehension. This is the skill that allows students to grasp meaning out of the information they hear and respond appropriately. The strategies you can apply to assist your students with developing listening comprehension include listening to information and responding verbally and nonverbally; listening for main ideas, details, and sequences; listening for implied meaning (include idiomatic expressions)' applying knowledge of vocabulary; discourse markers and organization; and tone.
