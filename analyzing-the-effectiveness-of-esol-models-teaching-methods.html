Teaching English for Speakers of Other Languages (ESOL) includes a few different research-based models. This lesson explores four of the most relevant ESOL models and takes a look at their overall effectiveness.

A Bit of History

Let's go back in time. As you know, many immigrants arrived in the United States during the 17th and 18th centuries. With immigrants from all over the world, we can imagine people who spoke lots of different languages. This reality was the basis for how teaching English happened back then; many schools had a bilingual program.

However, it was only back in the 1960's that the U.S. government officially expressed through law that programs to teach English as a second language were needed. From then on, schools adopted different bilingual programs to teach English to all immigrants. As a teacher of English language learner (ELL) students, however, you know that there are different models to teach the language.

In this lesson, we get to meet Carol, who is now retired, but experienced different models to teach English for Speakers of Other Languages (ESOL). An ESOL model is a teaching approach based on research that applies specific strategies to make sure students learn English. Let's now take a look at the models.

ESOL Models Effectiveness

Before we begin, let's clarify an important aspect: the effectiveness of different ESOL models is a matter that linguists still discuss and there aren't definite answers. The reason is that different ESOL models can be effective depending on a number of factors, such as the students' abilities, their previous knowledge, the school environment, and so on. Some linguists even believe that a combination of ESOL models can be effective. Let's learn about four of those models, looking at the research basis and effectiveness of each one.

Pull-Out Model

The ESOL Pull-Out Model has this name because students who need to learn English as a second language are, literally, pulled-out of their regular daily classes to attend an English language session with a specialized instructor. Carol tells us that the research basis for this model is the argument that English learners need to receive specific teaching sessions that provide them with awareness of specifics of the English language. The methodology to teach such specifics includes grammar, vocabulary, speaking/listening practice, and reading/writing strategies.

Carol says this model often causes concern because parents might feel like their children are being discriminated against because they get pulled-out of the regular classroom. However, linguists consider this model to be particularly effective for learners in elementary school because they're usually at the first stages of learning the language and need more attention than other advanced learners.

Embedded in the Classroom Model

The ESOL Embedded in the Classroom Model involves having a teacher of English learners working along with a subject content teacher. Teachers also know this model as ''ESL Class Period.'' The research basis for this model is that English learners can learn important content (e.g. math) if they stay in a regular classroom while also learning English with the assistance of a specialized teacher.

Carol has had school jobs that apply this model; she says this practice sometimes causes a reaction in parents who argue that their children feel very self-conscious because a teacher is there to help them. Moreover, they argue that sitting all ELL students together in a group so the ELL teacher can assist them is rather segregationist. Despite this view, this model seems to be particularly effective for students in middle and high school because they need to attend to important subject content while advancing their English language skills.

Sheltered Instruction Model

The ESOL Sheltered Instruction Model teaches academic content to ELL students who attend to such content along with native speakers of English. The subject teacher uses simple language in order to involve ELL students in the learning process. The research basis for this model is that ELL students can develop language skills if they are immersed in an English-speaking environment in which they receive sheltered instruction. Thus, this set of strategies is used to involve learners in a learning environment that mimics that of native speakers.

Carol says this model works great if the subject teacher is aware of the language limitations of ELL students, and knows instructional strategies that aid them, such as asking questions to check for comprehension, developing vocabulary, and encouraging in-class participation. This model seems to be effective in the long-term, but does cause a bit of a struggle at first, specially for ELL students who are beginners.

Bilingual Program Model

The ESOL Bilingual Program Model allows ELL student to take subject content classes in their native language and, in addition, take English language classes to develop their knowledge of this language. Within this model, there are schools that apply the transitional model, which means that once ELL students are comfortable enough with English, they transition into a full education in English, and other schools, who apply the dual model, which means ELL students keep learning in both languages so that they become fully proficient in English without losing their mother tongue.

The research basis for bilingual programs is that students who already speak a first language other than English, have an asset and, thus, they should be allowed to learn in their mother tongue while also learning English. Carol says she once worked in a school with a bilingual program, where she saw students feel confused at the beginning, but once they became comfortable with English, they were fine. The effectiveness of bilingual programs seems to be particularly evident for beginner students who benefit from taking their time to become comfortable with English, plus they get to maintain their mother tongue.

Lesson Summary

Let's recap! There are many different models to teach English for Speakers of Other Languages (ESOL). An ESOL model is a research-based teaching approach that applies specific strategies to ensure students learn English. There are not definite answers regarding the effectiveness of different ESOL models, though perhaps a combination of models can work in different circumstances. The ESOL models we covered in this lesson include the Pull-Out Model, the Embedded in the Classroom Model, the Sheltered Instruction Model, and the Bilingual Program Model.
