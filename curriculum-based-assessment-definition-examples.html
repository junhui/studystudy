What is Curriculum-Based Assessment?

The curriculum-based assessment (CBA) definition is the regular testing of student skills based on the expectations of the school's curriculum. When teachers use curriculum-based assessment, they test each student regularly, often weekly, and then compare the results with the expected progress for the grade level. Not only do these assessments help track student progress, but also the teacher effectiveness and quality of the school.

Curriculum-based assessment is different from summative assessment because it does not occur at the end of a learning period. CBA is considered a version of progress monitoring where students are monitored regularly throughout a learning period. CBA is also referred to as curriculum-based measurement (CBM) because it measures progress against baseline expectations.


This student is taking a CBA on his letter and vowel fluency. He will take this regularly, and his progress will be tracked.

The Process of Curriculum-Based Assessment

The goal of CBA is to use the same assessment framework and monitor students on the yearly benchmarks for the entire grade level starting from the beginning of the year and lasting all year long. In theory, the average student would begin the year on the low end and have mastered all the skills from the grade-level curriculum. There are six steps to the process, and each one is important to follow to get the most accurate results. These steps include:

  • Teachers select a framework or learning probe that assesses a specific skill. The probes are short and easy to administer regularly and should be based on the standards and expectations of the end of the grade level year.
    • Reading, for example, could be short passages that assess fluency, accuracy, and self-correction. A math probe might cover general computation skills like addition or multiplication.
  • The teacher administers the probes on a regular schedule. The most frequent is weekly, and the least frequent should be monthly.
    • The probe should be administered and scored the same way each time to ensure continuity of results. The framework should be the same as well to ensure accurate results.
  • The student data results should be used to create a graph. The typical graph used is a line graph that should see a steady upward trajectory as the year progresses.
  • Teachers should set student goals based on the first couple of probes to understand where each student is and how they can reach the goal.
    • For instance, a student who starts significantly below grade level will probably not be expected to be on grade level by year's end but should show a year's worth of growth.
  • The data from the student graphs can be used to guide and inform the instruction process.
    • Teachers may need to change their approach, slow down their teaching, or focus on remediation based on the results of the probes through the year.
  • Communication is the key to ensuring that everyone is in the loop on student progress.
    • Parents and administrators must be aware of the results so that they can take any appropriate steps that might be needed and no one is left in the dark or blind-sided by student progress or lack thereof.

Using Curriculum-Based Measurement Data

Having lots of data points from assessments is excellent. However, they are entirely pointless unless action is taken based on the results. There are several different actions that educators can take to use the data in a meaningful way effectively. These actions include the following:

  • Guide Instruction: After the first few rounds of CBA, teachers can use the results to paint a picture of where the class is at the start of the year.
    • This data can help the teacher make individual goals for students based on where they started the year, but it also informs the teacher about how to approach the curriculum. A class where many students begin the year above grade level will require an instructional approach different from a class that starts below grade level.
  • Visualize the Data: Numbers tell a story, but graphics paint the picture. The best way to utilize the data effectively is to enter the results in a program that keeps a continually evolving graph for the teacher to track.
    • The teacher will gain much insight based on how the class-wide graph changes, especially on how individual graphs change. The graphs also help communicate the information to parents and administrators because it is easy to see the individual progress compared to the class and in comparison to the expected progress.
  • Individual Student Decisions: One of the most important aspects of CBA data is its insight into each student and their progress.
    • The data can help inform teachers about how to approach individual students. It can point to the need for severe remediation, additional enrichment, or specific areas of weakness or strength. The data can help teachers decide on reading groups, small group instruction, or classroom interventions.
  • Communication: Most teachers have a sense of where their students are working after several weeks of school, but it is hard to express that to others. Graphical data from CBA can be very effective at communicating to parents, administrators, and other faculty regarding student progress.
    • If a teacher believes that an Individual Education Program (IEP) might be needed for a particular student, the data from CBA can be extraordinary evidence to back them up. It is also an easy way to see the trends of a student when communicating with parents about their child's progress.


This graph shows the reading fluency of two students over five months versus the expected reading fluency for the grade.

Curriculum-Based Measurement Examples

In general, CBA's are short, regular, and consistent; consistency is the most important. If the assessments are not consistent in format, then data changes could be attributed to the test format and not the content. The following are some examples of CBA:

Subject Specific Area Description Procedure
Reading Fluency Reading fluency includes the rate students read, which is recorded as words per minute, and the accuracy. Accuracy is the percentage of words missed out of the total words read. A teacher will sit down with an individual student and have the student read a passage for one minute. The teacher listens as the student reads and marks words misread, skipped, and self-corrected. In the end, the teacher counts the total words read and the mistakes to come up with speed and accuracy.
Math Addition Addition fluency is an essential skill because it allows students to be more efficient and accurate when more difficult math is learned. Addition fluency is the speed and accuracy that a student can finish math problems. The teacher will pass out a math sheet containing a significant number of single-digit addition problems. They will set a timer for 3 minutes and allow the students to work on the problems. At the end of the three minutes, the students will stop. The teacher will grade each sheet and how many problems each student can solve in 3 minutes.
Language Arts Spelling Spelling CBA often involves a setlist of common words for a grade level. Each time the students take the spelling test, they spell the exact words. The teacher hands out a page with numbers on the side. The teacher will say the spelling word out loud, use it in a sentence, and repeat the word. The students will write the word next to the numbers.
Language Arts Writing Writing assessments are usually done using prompts. The teacher gives a quick prompt, and the students write for a predetermined amount of time. The critical thing about the writing prompt is its consistency, simplicity, and broadness. It should be used multiple times to see the progress but not be so specific that the students run out of ideas. The prompt does not have to be the same, but it should be close in style to be compared to other prompts.

One thing that is important to consider is the delivery and scoring method of the assessments. When possible, it is best to use technology to help with the scoring and the delivery because it takes less time away from classroom instruction and preparation. Certain CBA is more complex than others to migrate to digital, and assessments like reading fluency should always remain in-person. Another suggestion for scoring subjective assessments, like writing prompts, is to switch with a colleague. Often teachers tend to have biases towards certain students, and switching subjective assessments can eliminate any potential bias.

Lesson Summary

Curriculum-based assessment is a way for teachers to monitor and track student and class progress through regular testing of essential curriculum skills. The assessments, also known as probes, should be short, regular, and consistent, and a series of steps are followed to ensure consistency. For instance, a teacher will use or create the framework of the probes based on grade-level expectations, delivering the probes regularly to ensure accurate results. Once placed in a graphical format and used to deliver instruction and set individual goals, the teacher will use the data to communicate student progress to parents and administration effectively.

Curriculum-based assessment, also known as curriculum-based measurement, should guide instruction and intervention and are most effective in the core subjects like math, writing, and reading. The data collected from the assessments are used to decide classroom interventions and potential IEPs or other forms of student support.

What Is Curriculum-Based Assessment?

Think back to when you were a student in elementary school. You probably remember having tests every few weeks after you completed a unit in math, reading, science, or social studies. These tests would determine how successfully you learned the material from those units, and your score would go home to your parents. Then all of these scores would be averaged, and you'd receive a report card.

These kinds of tests can be useful for capping off a unit, but they fall short in showing changes in student progress. This is where curriculum-based assessment also known as curriculum-based measurement (or CBM) comes into play. CBM is the repeated, direct assessment of targeted skills in basic areas, such as math, reading, writing, and spelling, using materials taken directly from the teaching curriculum.

The Process of CBM

To understand how curriculum-based assessment works, lets consider the case of Mr. Smith, a third grade reading teacher. He has been using CBM in his classroom for several years and has found that it gives him a much clearer picture of student progress than previous methods. He still gives unit tests, projects, and quizzes. These types of assessments still have a place in the classroom and can provide additional information on the progress of each student.

But Mr. Smith uses CBM to more closely monitor his students' short term progress on specific, basic skills. He creates probes, or short activities or sets of questions that target a specific skill, which assesses students' progress. These probes are taken directly from curriculum materials and created by the classroom teacher, instead of from an outside company.

In his reading class, Mr. Smith uses several methods of CBM that are supported by research. For example, to test fluency, Mr. Smith randomly chooses short passages from a reading book in the curriculum. He then sits and has students come up one by one and read 3 passages, each for one minute. While the students are reading, Mr. Smith is marking mistakes the student makes on his own copy of the passages.

After a student reads from the passages, he calculates his or her score by finding out how many words the student read correctly from each passage and finds the middle score. He considers this the student's true score. Mr. Smith can then compare this score to previous scores by placing them on a graph, which gives a clear picture of the student's fluency development.

This is just one way teachers can use CBM in the classroom. It can also be used with math skills, spelling, and writing. The important thing is to ensure that probes are created using materials from the curriculum and administered exactly the same to every student.

For example, if you were teaching second grade math, you might use CBM to monitor progress on math fact automaticity. To do this, you could create a worksheet with a sampling of math facts. Every week, hand out the worksheet to students and give them three minutes to complete as many facts as possible.

After the three minutes are up, you could either collect the worksheets or have students check each other's work, determining how many facts they solved correctly. Students can then chart their own progress on a graph. This may be especially useful because it gives the class extra incentive to work on memorizing math facts, so they can see their graph go up.

As we've seen, CBM can be used in a variety of basic skill areas. The examples only discuss very specific skill sets, but CBM can be used to monitor progress in spelling, writing, and a variety of math skills. As long as the assessments are administered regularly and frequently, and they are taken from curriculum materials, you can use them to keep track of many areas of student learning.

Using CBM Data

Curriculum-based measurement allows teachers to gather important data about student learning on a weekly basis. This data is incredibly important for reflecting on your teaching and making instructional decisions. Using data to drive instruction is a key component of teaching, and CBM is a great tool for doing so.

For example, imagine a student named Mark in your third grade classroom. Mark took a reading fluency probe at the very beginning of the year, giving you a clear understanding of Mark's fluency level. After about three weeks of teaching, however, you notice Mark is not improving in fluency as he should be, compared to other students. This tells you it's time to try new strategies with Mark to help bring his fluency scores up and get him back on the right track. This type of monitoring is possible because of the regular, frequent assessment provided with CBM.

CBM data is also useful for sharing information with administration, parents, and other teachers about how your students are progressing. For example, if a parent is expressing concerns that his or her child isn't improving in reading comprehension, you can check your CBM data to determine if their concerns line up with classroom performance. If they do, you can adjust instruction. If not, you can reassure parents with clear evidence that their child is progressing normally.

Lesson Summary

Curriculum-based assessment , also known as curriculum-based measurement (or the acronym CBM), is the repeated, direct assessment of targeted skills in basic areas, such as reading, writing, spelling, and math. The assessments use material taken directly from the curriculum to measure student mastery. CBM is effective because of its reliance on regular, frequent assessments and administration of the same assessments to every student.

A common strategy is to administer small, frequent activities called probes. Implementing them consistently with all students gives the teacher a clear picture of how each student is progressing in his or her learning. This data can then be used to drive instruction, make decisions, and inform others outside the classroom about any student's performance. While it may seem daunting, CBM is incredibly useful and, with practice, can become a routine part of any teacher's curriculum.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the meaning of CBM?

CBM stands for curriculum-based measurement and is another term for curriculum based assessment. CBM is when a teacher probes student skills on a regular basis and compares the data to the expected progress to be made in the grade.

Which is a major advantage of curriculum-based assessment?

The advantage of curriculum based assessment is that it can help to monitor the progress of each student, provide data to compare to the baseline skills, give teachers data to inform instruction and set goals, and give valuable information to parents and administrators

What are examples of curriculum-based measurements?

One example of a curriculum based measurement is a monthly test of expected spelling words for the grade level academic year. Another example would be a 3 minute timed addition assessment to track the students' progress in math fluency.
