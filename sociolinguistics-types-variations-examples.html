What Is Sociolinguistics?

What is sociolinguistics? Sociolinguistics is a branch of study in the broader field of linguistics that is concerned with how language functions in society. Sociolinguists study how social factors impact people's language use and how language use impacts and is determined by people's positions in society. This sociolinguistics definition may seem to cover a lot of bases, but all of these elements are closely associated with one another and are essential to the work of sociolinguists. William Labov, an American linguistic researcher, is often credited with the creation of the field of sociolinguistics as it is known today. He emphasized the idea that variation is central to language and he also pioneered quantitative methods of analysis in the field.

Sociolinguistics Examples

Sociolinguistics can be applied to virtually any area of daily life because language is so central to human social experience. Sociolinguistic examples that linguists might study could include:

  • Large-scale national studies of linguistic variation across a country
  • Small-scale local studies of linguistic variation in a state or city
  • Studies of language variation on a particular axis, like gender or social class
  • Studies of language variation among particular groups, like immigrants or second language speakers

Language variations are the key to sociolinguistic study, and every society will have different kinds of variations. The primary thesis of sociolinguistics is that each individual's use of language is impacted by, and also impacts, every aspect of their social place in society.

Sociolinguistic Variations

Sociolinguists study language variations that can occur for a wide variety of reasons. People all speak differently from one another, and even change their speech in different situations to account for social factors. Understanding how speech varies according to factors like region, social class, race, relationships, and gender is essential when it comes to understanding how certain forms of speech can be stigmatized. Sociolinguistics, like all branches of linguistics, is a descriptive discipline, not a prescriptive one. This means that sociolinguists are concerned not with how people ''ought to'' speak, but how they actually do speak. There are many ways that language can vary, but crucially, no linguistic variation is better or worse than any other; none is more correct or more intelligent, and all forms of language are equally complex and rule-bound.

Variance by Region

Ethnography is a term for the study of culture, including social and speech patterns and variation within a culture. Sociolinguists often take part in ethnographic research to understand how language changes from one region to another. To understand variation by region, one need look no further than the United States. People raised in Ohio are likely to refer to a non-alcoholic fizzy drink as ''pop,'' while people raised in most parts of Pennsylvania would be more likely to refer to the same drink as ''soda.'' These changes in language use are caused primarily by geographical separation and to a lesser extent by cultural identity. People who live in different areas are more likely to develop different ways of speaking. These examples are all to do with vocabulary, which are usually the easiest differences to spot, but regional variation can also influence pronunciation, grammar, and syntax.

Variance by Social Class

People often speak differently when they belong to different social classes. This is due to a variety of factors. Sometimes, certain social classes tend to live in particular regions, meaning that regional and class-based variation gets muddled. In other cases, people from a particular social class will develop ways of speaking that define them culturally. While class-based linguistic variation exists in the United States, it is easier to see in places like England, which has both significant linguistic variation and a relatively rigid class system. Received pronunciation, often called RP, is the accent that the British royal family have, meaning that it is strongly associated with the upper classes. A Yorkshire accent, on the other hand, is associated with the working class, particularly since many parts of the north of England have traditionally been poorer regions with large-scale industrial production.

People tend to speak more formally in job interviews than they do at home

Another aspect of class-based linguistic variation is the formality register. Most people will subconsciously speak more formally (in a way that is generally associated with higher social class) in certain situations, like job interviews, but will speak less formally among friends and family. This can be understood as a form of class aspiration in addition to a sense of social pressure to speak a certain way.

Variance by Racial Group

In some cases, sociolinguists have noted linguistic variation among racial and ethnic groups. Understanding this concept requires keeping in mind the caveat that there are no intrinsic differences in people's speech based on race; these differences are cultural and due to socialization. When racial or ethnic groups have strong communities, have historically been somewhat isolated from other groups, and have strong cultural ties, they may develop specific dialects. African American English (AAE) is an example of such a dialect in the United States. This is a dialect strongly associated with Black people, but not all Black people speak AAE, and some people speak it who are not Black, depending on where they grew up and other social factors. Other examples of racialized dialects include the Reservation Accent and New York Latino English.

An important element of this kind of linguistic variation to keep in mind is stigmatization. It is very common for dialects associated with marginalized racial groups to be considered lower class, crass, or simply incorrect. This is not actually the case and is the result of linguistic racism; all ways of speaking a language are equally correct, but some are simply given more social weight than others.

Variance by Relationships

Linguistic variation caused by relationships is connected to the concept of formality register described above. People will speak differently to their boss than to their sibling, because some social relationships require more formality than others. A related concept is the solidarity register: people will speak differently to people with whom they feel they have a lot in common versus people who seem very different. Usually, but not always, formality and solidarity registers are inversely proportionate in a given linguistic interaction.

There are many different ways that people in different cultures speak to those around them depending on their relationships. In some places, for instance, it is considered polite to refer to people by their surnames until a certain level of familiarity is reached, while in other places, referring to someone by their surname could carry negative connotations, like comparing them to a servant or school child.

Variance by Gender

People of different genders typically speak differently even when other factors like region and social class are the same. As with variations by racial group, gendered language differences are not intrinsic but are instead the result of socialization and social expectation. In North America, and in many other parts of the world, young women tend to have their speech patterns altered by their friends to a much greater extent than young men do, possibly because of the different way that women socialize with each other. If girls' language use is largely determined by their peers, boys' speech is primarily learned from their parents, in particular their mothers. This means that young women are typically at the forefront of linguistic innovation in a given cultural group.

Young women are often at the forefront of linguistic innovation

Women also tend to be more willing and able to adjust their speech patterns to different contexts than men do. On the whole, women who move from one region to another may be quicker to change their accents and dialects to match their new home than men are. This is a general trend, but not a hard and fast rule; there are certainly many examples of the reverse being true, but the patterns, according to sociolinguistics, are clear.

Lesson Summary

Sociolinguistics is the study of language and how it is affected by a variety of factors like region, social class, and gender. When conducting their studies, sociolinguists often participate in ethnography research, which is the study of culture and social patterns, including speech patterns. One of the biggest factors that influences how people speak is the region where they were born. This can impact accent, vocabulary choice, and grammar.

Gender, social class, relationships, and even race can have an impact on people's speech. Sociolinguistics is concerned descriptive studies that explore how people actually speak, rather than prescriptive studies that focus on how they should speak. Although some kinds of speech are stigmatized more than others, sociolinguists have found that stigma this is the result of bias, racism, and discrimination, and that all kinds of speech are equally correct and complex.

Video Transcript

Sociolinguistics

In today's lesson, we're going to cover the topic of sociolinguistics, which is the study of language and how it is affected by region, social class, relationship, and even gender - in other words, how language varies from person to person and culture to culture. For this reason, this lesson will be much more anecdotal than some, focusing more on examples than concrete anthropological terms.

However, before we get started, there is one thing we need to note. Sociolinguists do not pass judgments on how language should or shouldn't be spoken. They are not like high school grammar teachers, deciding on acceptable and unacceptable forms of speech. They simply take a look at languages within their differing social contexts, seeing one as being just as valid as the other.

Variance Due to Region

To begin, let's take a look at how language varies across regions. According to sociolinguistics, the way people speak has very much to do with where they were raised. For example, I have a friend who is from a different region of the country than I am. Unlike me, who says 'How are you all doing?', she simply says, 'How are yins?' To be honest, the first time she said it, I had no clue what she was trying to ask me. However, I soon learned that 'yins' was just the way she grew up saying the word 'you' in its plural form. To me, it was a bit odd. However, to a sociolinguist, this stuff is golden, as they seek to understand the ethnography of her area. Using more common words, 'ethnography' means the study of culture, including cultural and social patterns of speech variation within a culture.

Jumping across the Atlantic, sociolinguists also study why Americans and English people, who share a common language, still vary greatly in the way they speak. For instance, when deciding to converse with someone over the phone, why do Americans say, 'I'm going to call her,' while the English tend to say, 'I'm going to ring her up'? Yes, they both carry the same meaning, but they vary in the actual language used.

Variance Due to Social Class

With differences in region, sociolinguists also cite differences in social class as contributing to language variation. For this one, we'll use the work of anthropologists Carol and Melvin Ember to explain. In their book, Cultural Anthropology, the Embers cite a study in which children from what are deemed higher classes tend to enunciate their words, specifically the ends of words, more than children from what are deemed lower classes. For instance, a child from an upper-class family will tend to say 'We are talking' instead of 'We are talkin'.'

Again jumping across the globe, this is noted in England, as people of lower socioeconomic status tend to drop certain letters from the beginning of sounds. For instance, they may say 'I'm going to my 'ouse' instead of 'I'm going to my house.' This is another example of how social class affects language variation.

Variance Due to Relationships

Next, we come to how relationships affect language variation. For this one, we'll use a study conducted by sociolinguists, Roger Brown and Marguerite Ford, which explored the use of surnames within relationships. In their work, they assert that the use of terms of address vary depending on the relationship between the speakers. For example, when people know each other very well and feel equal to one another, they use each other's first names. I never call up my friend and say 'Hello, Mrs. Miller.' I simply say 'Hi, Lisa!'

However, when I visit my doctor, who's actually pretty close to my own age and who I've also seen in social settings, I still greet her with a 'Hello, Dr. Myers,' while she in turn says, 'Hello, Jessica.' Although I've never thought of it this way, a sociolinguist would have a field day with this, as they'd explain it by saying that when one person feels the other has more power or status in a situation, they will tend to use surnames or titles when addressing them.

Variance Due to Gender

Moving away from relationships, sociolinguists also cite gender as a contribution to the variations in language use. For instance, again using the word 'talking,' many of sociolinguists' studies have found that American women tend to pronounce the full end of the word, whereas American men are much more likely to simply say 'talkin'.' However slight this difference is, it's still a variation somewhat linked to gender.

For more profound examples of language variation due to gender, we can take a look at traditional Japanese culture. Again using the work of the Embers, they assert that women in Japan actually add an 'o' to the beginning of their words, which in Japan signifies politeness. Although not empirically proven, many sociolinguists believe this stems from the ancient Japanese cultural paradigm that women should be submissive, speaking with humility at all times. Whether or not this is true, it still holds as an example of how gender affects language variation.

Lesson Summary

Sociolinguistics is the study of language and how it is affected by region, social class, relationship, and even gender. In the study of sociolinguistics, ethnography, or the study of culture, including cultural and social patterns of speech variation within a culture, makes up a good deal of its content.

When studying the effects of region on language, sociolinguists feel that where a person was raised causes much variation in language. Adding to this, a person's socioeconomic status also tends to determine how people actually pronounce words, as well as the actual words they choose to use.

Adding to this, language also tends to vary depending on the relationship between the two speakers. For instance, sociolinguists assert that when one person feels the other has more power or status in a situation, they will tend to use surnames or titles when addressing them.

Lastly, language variation can also be explained by gender. For example, women in Japan tend to speak in a way that still reflects the traditional Japanese paradigm of women being submissive within society.

Learning Outcomes

This lesson covers sociolinguistics and should help you to:

  • Define sociolinguistics
  • Analyze how different regions can change how someone speaks
  • Describe the variation in language between social classes
  • Consider how relationships may impact how someone speaks
  • List some of the differences between genders when it comes to language use

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic premise of sociolinguistics?

The idea behind sociolinguistics is that language is highly variable and that it is impacted by complex social factors. Sociolinguistics approaches language with the idea that all kinds of speech are equally correct and valuable, even though some are stigmatized.

What are the types of sociolinguistics?

Sociolinguistics is not generally divided into sub-groups, but there are several kinds of variation that sociolinguists study, including:

  • Variation by social class
  • Variation by region
  • Variation by gender
  • Variation by racial group
  • Variation by relationships

What is sociolinguistics and examples?

Sociolinguistics is a branch of linguistics that studies how social factors impact language use. Examples of sociolinguistic study include gendered language differences, regional differences, and how social class impacts language use.
