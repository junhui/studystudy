If you are a teacher who works with English language learners, then you might be looking for ways to help increase your students' fluency. This lesson discusses some phonological and morphological skills that will help your students' speech.

Understanding Phonology and Morphology

As a teacher of English language learners (ELLs), Daria knows that it is important for her to understand the different components of language her students are thinking about. This year, she is working with several students who are brand new to speaking English.

As a result, she has started to brush up on her understanding of phonology and morphology. Daria understands that phonology refers to pronunciation and the understanding of sounds in words.

Morphology, on the other hand, has to do with the components that make up the meaning of a word and the process of forming words. For example, a student might learn the word 'car,' which is a morpheme, or the smallest unit of language that contains meaning. The student will also learn the morpheme 's,' which turns car into cars, the plural form.

Daria has noticed that some students really struggle with phonology and morphology, and this, in turn, affects their fluency, or ability to speak with good pacing and expression.

Explicit Instruction in Phonics and Morphology

In phonics and morphology, students benefit from explicit instruction, which is direct, clear, and systematic. In other words, Daria knows she cannot simply expect every student to pick up on a pronunciation pattern from listening to her.

Instead, she directly teaches students what the sound is, how they should form their mouths, and what words they find the sound in. Similarly, she teaches her students explicitly about morphology, helping them understand what morphemes are used most commonly in English and how they can add these morphemes to change the meanings of words.

When Daria engages in explicit instruction in phonics and morphology, she starts with sounds and concepts that are easier for students and proceeds gradually to more difficult skills and concepts.

Decoding Instruction

Daria understands that when students struggle with phonology and morphology, they often have trouble reading in English. Their reading fluency is impacted by their decoding, or capacity to sound out words on the page. Therefore, Daria thinks about incorporating more explicit decoding instruction into her work with students as well.

Spelling Patterns

First of all, she uses visual cues, explicit instruction, and word games to teach her students about the most common spelling patterns in English. For example, she teaches her students about the 'ai' sound and words that include 'ain,' like pain, rain, and train.

Then, she has her students search for words that follow this spelling pattern in their independent reading. She asks them to unscramble the scrambled versions of these words, spell the words from magnetic letters, and incorporate the words into their own writing.

Sound-Symbol Codes

Daria also knows that her students are essentially learning to crack codes when they learn to read and spell in English. These sound-symbol codes have to do with how different letters symbolize sounds.

For example, Daria teaches her students explicitly that the letters 'igh' are altogether pronounced like a long ''i'' sound; now her students can decode this symbol ('igh') into a sound every time they see it in writing.

Learning about sound-symbol codes also aids Daria's students when it comes to orthography, or appropriate spelling in English.

Using the Student's First Language

Finally, Daria thinks it is so important to remember that while her students might not be fluent in English yet, most of them are indeed fluent speakers and, sometimes, even fluent readers in their native language.

Daria explains to her students and their families that they can use what they know about their first language to aid their fluency, decoding, and communication skills in English. For example, she listens to one student speaking Spanish and realizes this student has a deep understanding of how to smoothly pronounce consonant clusters.

Even Daria, who does not speak Spanish, can appreciate the fluency of her speaking. She encourages the student to record herself speaking Spanish, then use the recording to help her realize what she does when pronouncing clusters and how she can make her English speaking more fluent using these same techniques.

Lesson Summary

Helping students develop their skills in phonology (pronunciation and understanding of sounds) and morphology (units of meaning and word formation) in English goes a long way toward teaching them how to express themselves overall, especially when it comes to speaking and reading with fluency, or ease of expression.

It often helps to incorporate explicit (direct and systematic) instruction in phonics and morphology, getting more difficult as students become more advanced.

It can also be meaningful to teach students decoding strategies, which uses spelling patterns to sound out words, and sound-symbol codes, which are sets of letters that are translated into a specific sound. Finally, remember that what students know about phonology and morphology in their home language can go a long way toward moving them to fluency in English.
