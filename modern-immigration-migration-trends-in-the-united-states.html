The complex picture of modern immigration trends in the United States includes both authorized and unauthorized immigrants, immigrants who enter as refugees, secondary migrants, and transnational migrants.

Contemporary Migration Trends in the United States

In the global population, the U.S. represents about 5 percent, yet 20 percent of international migrants live in the United States, making it the biggest immigrant destination in the world. Many immigrants are family members of American citizens, but others are refugees, asylum seekers, or undocumented immigrants who cross the border illegally. The complex patterns of immigration to the United States also include secondary migrants, who come to the US from somewhere other than their home country, and transnational migrants, who maintain close ties to their home country after arriving in the US.

Who Immigrates to the United States: The Big Picture

Overall, immigrants make up about 13.9% of the US population, approximately 46.2 million people. That's not remarkably high, historically speaking: in 1890, the immigrant population reached 14.8% of the total US population. Over 1 million immigrants enter the United States every year. Over 75% of those immigrants currently reside legally in the U.S.

Immigrants are about 49% men and 51% women, and older on average than the native-born population, with an average age of 38. In 2022, the largest location of origin were the Americas with 52% of immigrants' birth origins being in North or South America. Following are roughly 31% from Asia, 10% from Europe, and 6% from Africa. On average, immigrants have a slightly lower level of education than native-born Americans, with fewer immigrants holding college diplomas but 75% having completed high school.

One major recent change in US immigration trends has been the geographical distribution of immigrants within the United States. Since the 1990s, immigrants have spread out to a greater number of states. In some parts of the US, like the Midwest, immigration is reducing population loss or even increasing the population.

Recently, immigrants have also brought greater linguistic diversity to the US. Many immigrant families speak a language other than English at home.

Different Groups of Immigrants

To understand the complex patterns of immigration into the United States, it helps to know a bit more about different groups of immigrants who often have different demographic characteristics and arrive in the country for different reasons.

Documented and Undocumented Immigrants

Documented immigrants make up about 75% of the United States' foreign-born population. In 2022, 53% of immigrants in the United States were naturalized with the highest numbers being immigrants from Europe (67%), Asia (63%), and the Caribbean (63%). Most documented immigrants are relatives of people who are already US citizens, which makes it easier to obtain a visa or green card. In 2022, more than one million immigrants became legal residents. The most recent data shows almost 18,000 asylum-seekers were granted asylum. A few people also enter through the diversity lottery, which allows immigration from countries that don't typically send many people to the US.

About 10 million foreign-born residents in the US are undocumented immigrants, who are in the country in violation of American immigration laws. There are many ways one can become an undocumented immigrant. Most unauthorized immigrants enter the country legally but remain in the US after their visa has expired. Most undocumented workers live with family.

Others, who are seeking jobs that can support their families or fleeing violence in their home countries, undertake the extremely dangerous journey to cross the border into the US. For the most part, undocumented immigrants do not receive government aid, such as scholarships, healthcare, or unemployment benefits. Undocumented immigrants can also face deportation if their immigration status is discovered.

There has been great advocacy for undocumented immigrants. The clearest example of this is the DREAM Act, which is a legislative proposal that would grant undocumented immigrants who arrived in the country before age 16 to attain permanent legal residency by meeting a set of requirements.

Refugees and Asylum-Seekers

If immigrants face a threat of racial or religious persecution in their home country, and if they meet certain other standards, they can enter the United States as refugees. Since the Refugee Act of 1980, about 3.2 million refugees have resettled in the United States. The annual number of refugees entering the US has fluctuated over time, from over 100,000 refugees per year in the early 1990s to a low of 27,000 after the 9/11 terrorist attacks. In 2022, President Biden set the ceiling for incoming refugees at 125,000, the highest in several decades.

As of 2024, close to 110 million people, the highest in global history, are displaced forcibly due to war, conflicts, genocide, or other factors. According to the U.S. Department of State, ''2.4 million refugees are now in need of protection through third-country resettlement. For these refugees, resettlement in the United States represents the opportunity to start anew to pursue a life of safety and dignity without fear of violence or persecution.'' In the last 43 years, the average number of refugees resettled in the U. S. per year is 73, 300.

According to the Migration Policy Institute, 43 % of refugees admitted in the U. S were from Africa, 28 % from the Middle East and South Asia, 13 % from East Asia, 11 % from Latin America and the Caribbean, and 4 % from Europe and Central Asia.

Secondary Migrants

Secondary migration refers to a multi-step migration process in which an immigrant takes an indirect route into the United States through an intermediary country. Approximately 12-15% of immigrants to the United States are secondary migrants.

A US greencard (permanent residency card)

Secondary migrants are much more likely than other migrants to enter the US either as refugees or on skill-based or employment-based visas. They have a higher average level of education and tend to find more lucrative jobs in the US.

Transnational Migrants

Not all immigrants leave behind their family ties to their home countries when they enter the United States. In transnational immigration, immigrants come to the United States to live in communities of other immigrants and remain in close contact with their countries of origin. Transnational immigrants might work in the United States but send money home to family members in other countries. This creates complex economic networks across continental lines.

Lesson Summary

As a country founded by immigrants, it should come as no surprise that the United States is the world's biggest immigrant destination, with immigrants making up 13.9% of the population. Immigrants bring linguistic, cultural, professional, and artistic diversity to the country, shaping it for the better.

There are various types of immigrants. Many possess legal documentation to live in the United States, but others are undocumented. Many immigrants come voluntarily, but others enter as refugees. Some immigrants are also secondary migrants, arriving in the US from a country that was not their native country, and others are transnational migrants, retaining strong ties to their home country.
