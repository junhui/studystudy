Identifying the Difficulty Level of Academic Language

This lesson provides ways for teachers to identify a student's difficulty level of academic language. Understanding Cummins' four quadrants and other strategies, teachers can prepare lessons that meet students where they are and move their learning forward.

Importance of Academic Language

Your students are learning fundamentals of language now that will serve them throughout their school career and life. It's important they build a solid foundation and learn key skills. Use the tips in this lesson to understand your students' progress.

Teachers have the daily challenge of reaching students where they are and presenting material in an accessible and engaging way. Ultimately, teachers want to encourage students to learn and meet the requirements of their grade level. Additionally, they want to ensure they are teaching at the appropriate difficulty level for language or content area. Academic language is the language that your students need to do their work in school.

ESL

You will likely encounter students who are learning English as their second language, who do not come to you with a fluent academic language. To teach English as a second language (ESL), you will need to adapt lessons to match the students' academic performance levels, while also supporting their study of English. ESL students need opportunities to learn vocabulary and grammar. Starting with the alphabet, students must learn letters, spelling, and reading. Gather resources, such as picture books, basic spelling games, and reading projects. This will allow the students to learn basic English skills that will build a foundation.

Content Area Difficulty

Key content areas that teachers must consider include English language arts, mathematics, science, and social studies/history. The most basic method for determining content difficulty is conducting standardized tests. This may be in the form of writing, multiple choice, or verbal tests. Ideally, you will schedule time to meet with each student periodically to review progress and gauge individual development. Additionally, you will want to offer a standardized way to check for understanding. Depending on the grade level you teach, you may find games or worksheets that evaluate students' understanding of concepts. Older students can be given more complex tasks, like projects or group work, to ensure all students are working within appropriate markers for their age group.

Strategies for Evaluating Progress

Evaluating the progress of all students can be done creatively and interactively. Use games, learning stations, and group activities to monitor the progress of your students. Not only will you see their academic progress, but you also can evaluate their communication and language skills as the students interact.

English, specifically, can be evaluated through writing and storytelling. Journaling is an easy and consistent way for students to write every day and demonstrate their skills. Younger students can draw pictures to support their writing skills. Labeling the pictures helps to strengthen verbal and language skills. They can also give narratives about what the picture shows, again strengthening their English and grammar skills. Older students can respond to prompts or questions that allow them to enhance their critical thinking and English skills. You can take their responses and find weaknesses or areas that need further attention in your class work.

When working with students, it is important to understand that a student may have a grasp of the English language in casual conversation. The same level of comfort may not exist in academic language, however. Testing your ESL students for BICS (Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills) and CALP (Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency) will ensure they have the English skills necessary to be successful academically.

Cummins' Four Quadrants

One tool teachers can use is Cummins' four quadrants, which evaluate relevance and challenge levels. With this tool, you can effectively consider your coursework and determine if it is fitting for your students.



Cummins' quadrants are broken into four categories: A, B, C, and D. Going from top to bottom you go from content that is cognitively demanding to content that is not cognitively demanding. Going from left to right you go from high context and relatable content to abstract and non-relatable content. So, B represents cognitively demanding, relatable coursework, and A represents not cognitively demanding but relatable coursework. Looking at the other side, C represents cognitively demanding but abstract or not easily relatable coursework. D represents not cognitively demanding, unrelatable coursework (busywork).

Ideally, your activities will start in quadrant A and move up and over as your student progresses in his or her learning as quadrant A represents an activity that the student can easily relate to but is not too challenging. Once students master this activity, you move them to quadrant B where the activities are still highly relatable but more challenging. After excelling in quadrant B, you continue to increase the challenge by moving students to quadrant C, offering work that is still challenging but also more abstract. Your students must stretch to comprehend the academic language and content at this level. If you have work that falls into quadrant D, you may be giving students work that is not challenging and doesn't have meaning or context. Some say this level of work is ''busy work'' and offers little value to students' learning and growth. Level D work may include basic worksheets or coloring, activities that are simple, and fill time more than enhance skills.

Strategies for Applying the Quadrants

To use the quadrants effectively to help your students learn and to ensure content-area tasks continue to expand your students' academic growth, ask yourself some key questions:

  • How do I move students from quadrants A to B and from D to C?
  • How do I help students understand the context of information in quadrant C?
  • How much time are my students spending in Quadrant D?
  • How can I minimize activities in quadrants D and A?

Your goal is to focus on Quadrant B — helping students to be challenged by and relate to the topics you're teaching.

As you consider the level of activities in relation to the quadrants, you will find a more meaningful way to analyze class projects, assignments, and activities. Your goal is to keep your students in Quadrant B as much as possible. This is the position where students are being challenged and the information they are learning has an understandable application in their lives.

Lesson Summary

Identifying the difficulty level of academic language, the language students need to succeed at school work, is challenging yet vital to your success as a teacher. By evaluating your information, projects, and assignments with Cummins' four quadrants, you can encourage your students while also giving them learning opportunities that have a clear context within their experiences. This practice reinforces their learning and helps them better grasp the concepts you are introducing. In the process of preparing your lesson plans, consider how you can help ESL students to understand and use English. Additionally, find creative and helpful ways to evaluate the progress of your students and see how effectively they use English when communicating with their classmates.
