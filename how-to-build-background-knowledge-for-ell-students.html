In this lesson, teachers will learn about the necessity of building background knowledge in English language learner (ELL) students. Teachers will learn specific strategies for implementing this approach.

Building Background

Imagine that you've been an aspiring chef for several years. You love experimenting with exotic ingredients, trying new cooking techniques, and developing recipes. You decide to take a cooking class to refine your skills.

On the first day of class, you sit next to Ana, a total beginner who has recently graduated from high school. Everything she's ever learned about cooking can be summarized in two recipes: boiled hotdogs and spaghetti.

When the cooking instructor asks students to make crème brûlée for their first assignment, Ana is at a disadvantage. She has little background knowledge about the culinary world. How could the instructor help prepare Ana for this assignment?

Making Meaning

One way that we make meaning of new information is by applying it to information that is stored in our brains. Without this background information, we'd have difficulty understanding much of anything. Consider the following example:

''It's been several decades since Neil Armstrong left his footprint and our red and white stripes on the lunar surface. The White House supports NASA's plan to send more astronauts to the moon.''

The average literate adult would likely have no problem understanding that text. What about people who did not grow up in North America? Even overcoming the language barrier may not help those students understand references to NASA, the White House, Neil Armstrong, or the American flag. To meet the needs of these students, you'd have to help them acquire background knowledge.

Figurative Language

Other kinds of meaning are learned through immersion, such as in social situations or by listening to others speak. For example, if you are a native English speaker, it is unlikely that you have ever had to read a textbook to learn about idioms and metaphors. These figures of speech are usually just picked up 'along the way.'

Idioms and metaphors are often problematic for ELL students because students cannot make sense of them by understanding the individual words that comprise them. For example, if you advise an ELL student to 'kill two birds with one stone,' he or she may question your intent to harm animals.

For this reason, in addition to helping students acquire background information, it is equally important to provide an immersive language experience. The following strategies can help immerse students in an English-speaking environment:

  • Encourage student collaboration, such as group projects and partner reading
  • Facilitate class-wide, small group, and paired discussions
  • Use a variety of multimedia during instruction, including videos, audio books, software programs, music, and magazines

Making Connections

Ask students to share their cultural experiences with others. For example, if you're reading a story featuring a child's birthday party, ask your ELL students how birthdays are celebrated in their native countries. This will help them make connections to the topic.

Students may learn better if they are able to connect learning to prior knowledge. For example, when teaching about inventions, you might research some inventors from students' native cultures. When teaching about a historical event, try comparing it to an event from ELL students' birth countries.

Picture Walks

It's important not to simply dive into a text without engaging students in pre-reading strategies. Prepare students for the text by doing a picture walk. Go through the text, showing students pictures and asking them to discuss them and make predictions based on the images. This is an ideal time to discuss any vocabulary or difficult concepts students may be unfamiliar with. This will set students up for better reading comprehension.

Gallery Walks

For lessons that do not involve text, or for text that has no pictures, you can try a gallery walk. Let's use the example of Ana's cooking class. The instructor prepares for the lesson by placing large, poster-sized chart paper in different locations throughout the room. In the center of each chart is a word or phrase related to the lesson. In Ana's class, the charts may be labeled: 'Favorite Desserts,' 'French Desserts,' 'Broiling,' and 'Custard.'

Students form small groups and each group stands in front of one of the papers. Each group writes words and phrases or draws images and symbols to represent the word or phrase in the center. For example, on the paper that says 'Broiling,' one student might write, 'crispy top,' and another might draw a picture of a torch. Some students may have more background knowledge than others, and that is okay. Students discuss, brainstorm, and teach each other about the key words and phrases.

After a few minutes, students will move to the next chart. They will see what their peers have written and drawn on the chart, and they will add to it. By the end of the activity, students have a chance to see all charts, share their experiences and information, and learn from one another.

Realia

Realia refers to objects from everyday life. Let's say you're teaching ELL students about vocabulary related to having a picnic. You could show students a picture of a family having a picnic, or you could bring in a picnic basket and fill it with fruits, sandwiches, cutlery, napkins, and sparkling grape juice. The 'real thing' is almost always more likely to stick with students and enhance their learning, which is important for building background knowledge. Examples of realia include maps, atlases, photographs, restaurant menus, theater tickets, and any other 'real' item that will help make the subject more concrete for learners.

Lesson Summary

Having background knowledge about subject matter helps pave the way for student success. Consider Ana, who had no culinary experience, but was asked to make a complicated French dessert during her first cooking class. Perhaps her instructor should have taken some time to familiarize Ana with background information about French cooking. She could ask students to make connections, do picture and gallery walks, and use realia to bring the lesson to life.
