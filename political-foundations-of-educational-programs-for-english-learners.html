Political Foundations of Educational Programs for English Learners

The teaching of English to English learners in schools in the United States has been a politically complex issue. This lesson discusses some of the political foundations of English instructional programs in the country.

Understanding English Learners

As a public school system teacher, Lisa has witnessed many changes in the politics and demographics of her educational setting over the course of her career.

Many of Lisa's students today are English learners, which means that they are still in the process of acquiring both interpersonal communication skills and academic language in English.

Because many of these students are immigrants or come from immigrant families, their participation in educational programming tends to get heavily politicized. Lisa is interested in learning more about the political perspectives and philosophies that have the potential to impact the curriculum and instruction she uses with English learners.

Political Perspectives on Bilingualism

One of the first things Lisa encounters is the variation in political perspectives on bilingualism, or fluency in two languages. When Lisa was getting her master's degree, she was wrongly taught that growing up bilingual could harm a child's development and cognitive capacity.

Today, psychologists and educators know that bilingualism does not impede students' development. In fact, there is some evidence suggesting that a child who speaks two languages from the start is more cognitively flexible and able to move fluidly between languages.

Lisa realizes that some of the stigma historically attached to bilingualism has to do with political views, particularly on immigration. In the United States, many people associate bilingualism with an influx of immigrants speaking a wide variety of languages. For some people with nativist beliefs, which dictate that immigrants are lesser in status than those born in the United States, bilingualism is threatening.

In other words, the politics of bilingualism are inextricably linked with the politics of immigration, which fluctuate depending on economic, political and geopolitical factors. This in turn influences the ways that teachers talk to and work with children and parents around language development and growth.

Heritage Language Movement

With so much political pressure opposing bilingualism, Lisa starts to wonder how students and families respond. She learns about the heritage language movement, a social and political movement that argues that children should have deep access to learning and working in the language that is spoken in their home.

Proponents of the Heritage Language Movement, for instance, maintain that if a child's family speaks only Spanish in the home, it is just as important for the child to become a fluent speaker and reader of Spanish as it is for the child to learn academic English.

This is also a politically charged argument, and it embeds the assumption that children are more emotionally and culturally secure if they have access to the language associated with their family and heritage.

Lisa understands that the Heritage Language Movement does not oppose children learning English; however, it prioritizes ongoing access to and communication in the heritage language. People involved in this movement are in favor of bilingualism and argue in favor of classes in heritage cultures.

They might argue that students can get some of their academic instruction, and certainly do reading, in the language associated with their heritage.

English-Only Laws

Lisa knows that in the United States, there is no official language, or language sanctioned formally by the government. At the same time, she discovers that 31 states and several US territories have English as their official language.

In these states and others, some people are in favor of English-only laws and policies, which require that students and teachers speak only English in schools. Lisa can see many ways that these laws might be detrimental to her students, and might even keep families from communicating openly with children's teachers.

At the same time, nativists tend to think that English-only laws will lead to faster assimilation to US society and culture. Strident believers in these views lobby strongly for the exclusion of heritage languages from curriculum and instruction, and argue that English language learners should have the primary goal of becoming fluent in English as quickly as possible.

Lesson Summary

Many of the issues involved in teaching English learners, or children who are still acquiring English, are heavily politicized. This is largely because English acquisition has become tied up socially and politically with the overall issue of immigration.

Some people oppose bilingualism (fluency in two languages) in children, but there is no research to show that it's in any way detrimental.

The heritage language movement argues that children should always have access to strong instruction in the language spoken in their home. English-only laws argue that schools should do instruction only in English.
