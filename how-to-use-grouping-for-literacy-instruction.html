Instructor Rita Kerrigan

Rita has taught elementary and middle school and has a master's degree in reading education.
Grouping students is an effective teaching practice for literacy instruction. Learn about four types of grouping strategies and the benefits for learners.

Instructional Grouping

Instructional grouping, by definition, is when a large classroom of students is divided into smaller groups based on various criteria. This type of grouping is important in all aspects of education due to the increase of social interaction that exists when using small groups, as well as the motivational factor. Kids often respond more enthusiastically and openly when working in a small group as opposed to a large group setting.

Instructional grouping is especially important when dealing with literacy instruction due to the wide range of reading levels that exist within a traditional classroom. There are various ways to group students in a literacy classroom. Some of the most successful and frequently used are heterogeneous grouping, dynamic grouping, cooperative learning, and peer tutoring.

Heterogeneous Grouping

Heterogeneous grouping is when students of all different abilities are placed in a class together. The teacher may use shared reading, mini-lessons, or a read aloud to introduce concepts to the whole class but will do the bulk of literacy instruction in small groups due to the variety of levels present in a heterogeneous class.

In these small groups, students are grouped by reading levels for activities, such as guided reading. Guided reading is when the teacher leads a small group through an instructional level text while working on decoding and comprehension strategies. It is important to use these small groups that are based on reading ability when there are students of many different levels heterogeneously grouped in a classroom.

The teacher may also use one-on-one conferencing with students as a way to individualize instruction. A final commonly used practice of heterogeneous grouping is literature circles. A group of students reads a book on their reading level simultaneously and meets on a regular basis to discuss topics and concepts that arise in the book, similar to a book club. The teacher meets with groups on a rotating basis to aid with facilitation when needed and assess students.

Dynamic Grouping

Reading groups should always be dynamic. Dynamic grouping means that groups are not permanent. Rather, they can and will change often as the levels of the students also change. Benchmark assessments, such as running records, are used to place students into groups for a period of time. Teachers then can work with students of similar abilities on specific strategies in which they have demonstrated a weakness.

The guided reading groups previously discussed should always be dynamic in addition to heterogeneous. The assessments that are used to group students should be repeated often so that groups can be changed as needed as students' levels evolve at different rates. It is not recommended that dynamic groups be given a permanent name, as that could confuse students when they move into another group. Rather, it is best to use the individual names of the students or the current book they are reading, instead, to identify the group.

Cooperative Learning

Cooperative learning is when students learn through social interactions with each other, often in small groups or partners. These groups are formed based on different factors for various situations. They may be based on skill level, or they may have students with a range of skill levels work together. When there are various skill levels present, all learners benefit because the higher levels take on teacher roles and the lower levels can use their peers as models. Aside from the academic gain that cooperative learning provides for students, equally important is the social aspect of the grouping. Students learn strategies that involve cooperation, negotiation, and responsibility. These are lifelong skills that will help them become successful individuals long after they leave the walls of the literacy classroom.

Peer Tutoring

A literacy classroom often contains students that are reading at a high level mixed in with students who are reading at a low level, as well as everything in between those two vastly different levels. A recommended practice, peer tutoring involves partnering a student who fully grasps a concept with one who does not understand that concept. The student who has a clear understanding teaches the other student.

This practice is successful for many reasons. The student who takes on the teacher/tutor role is able to use his or her knowledge to explain the ideas in child-friendly language to the other student. Sometimes, all it takes is hearing the information in simpler words for a child to learn a concept. Also, the higher level student is challenged by becoming the teacher in the pairing. This offers that student the chance to learn and grow as well and stay motivated in the classroom. Peer tutoring is a wonderful way to group students in a literacy classroom because it allows both students the opportunity to grow as readers and writers despite being at different levels, while also giving them each self-confidence as learners in general.

Lesson Summary

It would be impossible to create a group of students who are learning at the exact same level in a traditional literacy classroom. Therefore, the teacher needs to use various instructional grouping strategies to reach all of the learners in the class on their individual levels.

Heterogeneous grouping uses small groups to divide students by level so that the teacher can work with a smaller group of students on skills they need to practice. The process of dynamic grouping makes sure that students are assessed regularly and that these groups change, due to the fact that students learn at different rates and their levels change often as a result.

Cooperative learning is a strategy where students work together on a concept to improve their skills, as well as their ability to interact socially. Peer tutoring pairs students of higher abilities to teach concepts to students of lower abilities. Utilizing instructional grouping in a literacy classroom is invaluable and should be something that every teacher makes a priority to reach every learner in his or her classroom.
