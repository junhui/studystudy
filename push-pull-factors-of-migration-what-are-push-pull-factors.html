Human Migration

The definition of migration refers to the movement of individuals, either seasonal or long term, from one place to another for various reasons. These migrations include migration of displaced persons, people moving for employment or to find better experiences, migration of refugees, and family reunification. For instance, approximately 245,000 people migrated to the U.S. in the year 2021. On the other hand, upon the onset of Russia's invasion of Ukraine, approximately 6.2 million people have migrated from Ukraine to other regions due to the war and political instability in Ukraine.

Human migration can be an individual, group, or a family type of movement. An individual may decide to move from one place to another because of personal issues like seeking better opportunities. Also, a group of people may decide to move because of internal war or drought. In this case, they move to seek refuge and increase their chances of survival before going back once the situation improves. However, some of the people in these groups may decide to remain in the new location if they find new opportunities or are given citizenship. Families may move to another place in order to unite with other family members.

Any type of migration is either voluntary or forced. Voluntary factors are when a person decides on their own to move from one location to another such as for better employment opportunities. Conversely, forced factors, such as war, occur when people are left with little option but to flee a region for safety purposes. Overall, people might move because of economic, social, political, demographic, environmental and physical reasons:

  • Economic factors of migration include job opportunities, income, and consumer or producer prices.
  • Social factors include search for education and family obligations.
  • Environmental factors include exposure to hazard, food or water security, energy security, and land productivity.
  • Political factors include conflict, persecution, and discrimination.
  • Demographic factors incorporate disease prevalence, population structure, and population density.
  • Physical factors include variables such as natural disasters.

All the above factors can comprehensively be classified as push or pull factors.

What Are Push and Pull Factors?

The definition of push factors are forceful reasons that cause people to leave their residence. They include aspects such as:

  • War
  • Poverty
  • Floods
  • Droughts
  • High crime
  • Lack of safety
  • Lack of services

For instance, a person may move to another country or state because they believe there are better opportunities to improve existing talent or start new business. Also, the strict measures enforced by the local government may be a contributing factor of moving to a new location. Additionally, war may cause people to seek refuge in another country until the situation is resolved. For instance, the Ukraine migration is primarily caused by war.

The definition of pull factors entails the reasons individuals move to a certain location. This includes variables such as:

  • Higher employment opportunities
  • More wealth
  • Safety
  • Political stability
  • Good climate
  • Better services
  • Low occurrence of natural hazards among others

For instance, a country may have better policies or governance that may attract people from different nations. This can be seen in the migration to the U.S., which is primarily caused by better employment opportunities. Moreover, students may prefer a certain destination because of its better learning infrastructures or facilities compared to home institutions. Overall, the ability of a country to provide better services or living conditions would definitely attract people in other nations trying to better their lives.

Examples of Push and Pull Factors

There are many examples that can be used to explain push and pull classifications. Push factors incorporate aspects that make people leave their country or a certain region. For example, war is among the most common reason for people to leave their own country to seek refuge in another. The war between Ukraine and Russia has forced many Ukrainians to leave their country and seek shelter or refuge in neighboring countries like Poland. Also, drought can cause people to migrate to other countries. For example, in the 19th century, the Irish people had to seek refuge in the United States because of famine in their country to avoid starvation. Another push factor is threat on personal life because of opposing religious or ethnic activities. For example, Jews were forced to leave Nazi German because of constant threats due to their religious affiliations.

Pull factors are the reasons that make people want to go to new destinations. For example, many people move to the U.S. because it offers better employment opportunities. Students also move to the U.S. because they have better learning institutions, such as Harvard. The students prefer studying in these universities because they also have a higher chance of getting employed immediately after graduation. Good climate can also act as a reason for wanting to migrate to a certain location. For example, a person may decide to migrate from Finland to Dubai because of the latter's warm weather. Additionally, certain countries are attractive because of their political stability. For instance, many people move to Canada because of its stable political stability, which ensures safety. Besides, people may move to Iceland or Switzerland because they are among the best global peace index, signaling how safe and peaceful it is to live there. Pull factors are driven by preference rather than the need or force to migrate.

Lesson Summary

Migration refers to the movement of individuals, either seasonal or long term, from one place to another. People might move because of economic, social, political, demographic, environmental, and physical reasons such as natural hazards. Comprehensively, these factors are classified as either push or pull factors. Push factors incorporate reasons people want to leave a region, while pull factors incorporate reasons people want to move into a certain region. Examples of push factors include war, political instability, famine, and drought, among others. Examples of pull factors include political stability, lots of jobs, natural resources, better learning institutions, and better climate. Ultimately, migration happens because of the combination of push and pull factors.

What is Human Migration?

Humans might be creatures of habit, but historically we tend to move around a lot. Most families in the USA, for example, did at one time live elsewhere. Human migration is the seasonal or long-term movement of humans from one area of the Earth to another. For example, if you move from the United States to Europe, you have just migrated. Or if you travel across the country to find a summer job, you also have just migrated. Or if your family were, several generations ago, from the country of Poland, your family is said to have migrated (or immigrated, which is a similar word).

There are many possible reasons that humans might migrate: economic reasons, social reasons, and physical reasons. Economic factors include things like searching for jobs or higher pay. Social and physical reasons usually involve forcible migration, like fleeing from persecution or intolerance or a natural disaster.

But reasons for migration can also be split into push or pull factors. Today we're going to talk in more detail about this way of categorizing the reasons for human migration.

Push Factors

A push factor is a reason for migration that relates to the area the person comes from - something that is pushing them out of an area. Examples of push factors include economic factors, like a lack of economic opportunity and jobs. For example, some people migrate from Mexico and Central America seasonally in search of agricultural work in the United States.

But there are also social factors, like escape from religious or cultural persecution, such as the Huguenots in 16th Century France and Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany. And then there are physical push factors, like natural disasters - towns and villages being destroyed by volcanoes, hurricanes, flooding, drought, and earthquakes. Wars also tend to lead to a lot of push factors - most wars lead to large numbers of refugees.

Pull Factors

A pull factor is a reason for migration that relates to the area of destination - something that is attracting them to an area. Examples of pull factors include an area being economically prosperous - having lots of jobs or a particularly large amount of freedom, making it a haven for those who are escaping from religious or cultural persecution. Sometimes this can be accidental, but countries have at times advertised themselves specifically to encourage migration. For example, a lot of countries were short on workers after World War II.

An area can also be economically attractive if it has lots of natural resources - for example, finding gold and oil attracted people to the west of the USA; both businessmen looking to make money and miners and engineers looking for work. A thriving new industry can also be attractive. Then there are environmental pull factors, like a pleasant climate.

Examples of pull factors include an area being economically prosperous, having a particularly large amount of freedom, a lot of natural resources, thriving new industry, or a pleasant climate.

Learning Outcomes

After you've completed this lesson, you should have the ability to:

  • Define human migration
  • Identify economic, social and physical reasons for migration
  • Differentiate between push factors and pull factors and provide examples of each

Prompts About the Push and Pull Factors of Human Migration:

Definition Prompt:

In at least two to three sentences, define human migration in your own words and provide at least one example of it.

Example: If you moved from Michigan to Louisiana, then you migrated from one part of the country to another.

Graphic Organizer Prompt 1:

Make a poster, chart, or some other type of graphic organizer that defines push factor and shows the different categories of push factors (economic, social, physical, wars). Make sure your graphic organizer depicts at least one example of each category of push factor.

Example: For a physical push factor, you could draw a hurricane.

Graphic Organizer Prompt 2:

Create a poster, chart, or some other type of graphic organizer that defines pull factor and illustrates the different reasons behind pull factors. Remember, these include economic reasons, attempts to find greater personal or religious freedom, access to natural resources, and environmental factors.

Example: You could draw an oil field with a magnet pulling people toward it to illustrate this type of economic and natural resource pull factor.

Letter Prompt:

Pretend you have decided to migrate somewhere. Write a letter to a friend explaining where you're migrating from, where you're migrating to, and the kinds of push factors and pull factors that have led you to make this decision. Your letter should be about one page in length.

Example: You've decided to migrate from Maine because you do not like cold weather and snow, and also don't have many friends there (physical and social push factors). Your chosen destination is Florida because of the warmer climate and because you think you'll be able to make friends on the beaches there who share similar interests as you (environmental and social pull factors).

What are pull factors?

Pull factors in migration are preferences or desires that cause people to migrate to another country. For example, better employment opportunities might attract someone to the U.S.

What are examples of push factors in migration?

War is an example of push factors in migration. It forces people to flee from their country to seek refuge in another. Also, famine forces people to leave their region or country in fear of dying of starvation. Threat on personal life may be a reason for migrating to another country. Finally, political instability and tension would force people to migrate in fear of civil war.

  • Human migration described
  • Moving away from an area due to a natural disaster

  • Identifying lists of potential pull factors
