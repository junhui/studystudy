English Language Development (ELD) programming serves as an essential component of increasingly diverse modern school systems. This lesson takes a close look at the critical aspects and characteristics of a clearly articulated ELD program within the context of the United States.

Naty's New Project

As the school's newly appointed English language program coordinator, Naty is charged with designing and implementing a robust and systematic program to help the English language learner population. Naty is not sure how to begin and is only minimally familiar with this sort of work. Let's take a look at some ideas for Naty to consider as she moves forward with program development and implementation.

An ELD program must consider not only materials, resources, and teaching methods, but also best practices such as cultural awareness.

What is an ELD Program?

An English Language Development (ELD) program is a carefully articulated and comprehensively implemented school program that supports English language learners. Although this sort of programming is often implemented around the world, this lesson considers language learners in the context of English speaking nations, in particular those attending school in the United States. Similar programming may also be used for very young native English-speaking students (as they are considered to be English language learners), as well as language-challenged students such as those with developmental disorders or other disabilities.

Naty does some research and begins to realize that the primary aim in ELD programming is to assist students as they develop the four main language skills of listening, speaking, reading, and writing. This includes supporting the development of technical sub-skills such as grammar, vocabulary, phonology, morphology, syntax, and fluency. Additionally, ELD programs may integrate cultural education; examples might include teaching students about American holidays, history, sports, etc.

Leveling Language Proficiency

There is a great variety of language proficiency models in the world of English teaching and learning. Schools may have the autonomy to adopt one or another model according to their needs and available resources.

However, one widely recognized model for determining the proficiency level of learners consists of five classifications: beginner, lower intermediate, intermediate, upper intermediate, and advanced. These levels utilize various descriptors and criteria, and may be interpreted and applied according to a school's unique context. It is important to also notice that a learner may be on one level with one skill (e.g. intermediate in listening) but on another with a different skill (e.g. lower intermediate in speaking).

Resource Considerations

Resources include materials, funding, teachers (including their skills, trainings, and methodologies) and auxiliary support systems such staff who are trained as English language learning specialists.

Naty understands that English language learners come from a variety of backgrounds. For example, even though the student population may have a mother tongue of Spanish, Naty knows that these students represent a number of Spanish-speaking nations, and thus a number of distinct cultures and Spanish language dialects. For this reason, Naty is careful to select materials that are suited for a diverse student population without making assumptions or generalizations about students' backgrounds.

Regarding teachers as resources, Naty decides to review faculty profiles and arrange one-on-one interviews with those who will be working with English language learners. She learns that some faculty are more suited to work with this population according to their respective skills, trainings, and methodologies. For example, some teachers may have more experience working with language learners or may be language learners themselves (so they understand the process first-hand); some may have formal certificates or degrees in language instruction; others may be particularly well-versed in differentiating instruction versus traditional lecture-and-textbook style teaching. For faculty who are lesser prepared, she begins to plan professional development training opportunities to strengthen the faculty as a whole.

Naty also takes inventory of available auxiliary resources and learns that some of the school's non-teaching staff members have specialized credentials and experience working with English language learners. It is determined that these professionals will be considered for integration into the ELD program.

Implementing the ELD Program

ELD programming has traditionally been confined to specialized yet isolated language support classes. In today's era, Naty understands that ELD programming must be more comprehensive and systematic. This means that language support must be integrated into content courses such as math, science, social studies, etc. Programming is often visible in professional development trainings for faculty. Additionally, ELD programming may involve parental involvement and community outreach. For example, a teacher may supplement classroom instruction by assigning an out-of-school project where students explore English by interviewing family members and local residents and then later reporting their findings to classmates.

Another effective tactic to consider is the use of authentic materials such as current news articles, novels, magazines, music, TV, movies, and technology-based programs that allow students to interact with native speakers of English. This allows students to experience the English language in real contexts outside of standardized and commercialized materials designed specifically for schools (e.g. textbooks).

Lesson Summary

This lesson provides an overview of English Language Development (ELD) programming (a carefully articulated and comprehensively implemented school programming that supports English language learners) and outlines key characteristics and components of such programming. Leveling (the process of identifying a learner's proficiency level), resources (funding, materials, and faculty backgrounds), and recommendations for implementation such as embracing a comprehensive and systematic approach, as well as utilizing authentic materials, are discussed. In the end, Naty was able to successfully learn how to design and integrate an ELD program in her school with a highly diverse student population.
